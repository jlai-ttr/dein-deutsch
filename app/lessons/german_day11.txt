NEW VOCABULARY (10 words)
--------------------------
1. der Morgen — morning

3. der Abend — evening
4. die Nacht — night
5. der Vormittag — late morning
6. der Nachmittag — afternoon
7. die Guten — (in greetings) "good"
8. Tschüss — bye (informal)
9. Auf Wiedersehen — goodbye (formal)
10. die Frage — question

GREETINGS BY TIME OF DAY
-------------------------
- Guten Morgen (07:00-10:00, until ~noon) — Good morning
- Guten Tag (10:00-18:00, but used most of the day) — Good day / Hello
- Guten Abend (18:00-22:00) — Good evening
- Gute Nacht (going to bed, 22:00+) — Good night

GOODBYES
--------
- Tschüss / Tschüß — informal, among friends
- Auf Wiedersehen — formal, polite
- Bis später — see you later
- Bis morgen — see you tomorrow
- Bis dann — see you then

SAYING YOUR NAME
-----------------
"Ich heiße [name]." — My name is [name].
"Ich bin [name]." — I'm [name].
"Mein Name ist [name]." — My name is [name].

GREETINGS IN A FULL CONVERSATION
----------------------------------
A: Guten Tag! Ich heiße Anna. Und Sie?
B: Guten Tag, Anna. Ich bin Max.
A: Freut mich! (Pleased to meet you!)
B: Freut mich auch! (Pleased to meet you too!)

WHEN TO USE "DU" VS "SIE"
---------------------------
- "du" = you (informal, family, close friends, children)
- "Sie" = you (formal, strangers, work colleagues, anyone older)

Default to "Sie" until invited otherwise.
German switches to "du" with: "Wir können 'du' sagen" or "Duzen wir uns?"

FIRST CASE: AKKUSATIV (ACCUSATIVE)
-----------------------------------
The accusative marks the DIRECT OBJECT — what is being acted upon.

"Ich sehe DEN mann." (I see THE man.)
"Hast du DAS buch?" (Do you have THE book?)

ARTICLE CHANGES (Nominativ → Akkusativ):
| Gender    | Nominativ | Akkusativ |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| maskulin  | der       | DEN       |  ← ONLY masculine changes
| feminin   | die       | die       |
| neutral   | das       | das       |
| plural    | die       | die       |

Mnemonic: "Der Mann → den Mann" — only masculine articles change.

"Akkusativ" comes from Latin ("accusation" = accusation = what you're charged WITH).
The direct object is WHAT something is happening to.
